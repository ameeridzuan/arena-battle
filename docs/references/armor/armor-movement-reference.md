# Human movement in armor: reusable character reference

Recorded: 19 October 2026. Scope: human characters, mainly unpowered armor. Research compiled with a delegated research agent.

This is a research and reconstruction guide, not newly filmed footage, measured motion capture, or a validated human simulation. Numbers marked **example** are design inputs; numbers marked **measured** belong only to the cited experiment. The blank character record accompanies this guide.

## 1. Evidence to preserve

| Finding | Evidence and limits | Character implication |
|---|---|---|
| Substantial mobility is possible in field plate | The Met describes typical complete field harness as roughly 20–25 kg, including a 2–4 kg helmet. Articulation and fit matter. [Museum reference](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/arms-and-armor-common-misconceptions-and-frequently-asked-questions) | Start with coordinated human motion; inspect each joint's actual clearance. |
| Effort can rise much more than appearance changes | Four experienced interpreters wore replicas averaging 35 ± 5 kg, about 44% of body mass. At matched speed, net metabolic cost was 2.1–2.3 times unloaded walking and 1.9 times running. These are energy-cost ratios, not speed ratios. Small treadmill study. [Askew et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3248716/) | Separate immediate movement capability from how long it can be maintained. |
| Harness-specific restrictions vary by action | A single-subject proof of concept reported similar hip/knee walking excursions but restrictions during some maximal functional movements, including shoulder flexion. One fit and one trained wearer cannot define everyone's joint limits. [Jaquet et al., author manuscript](https://artanim.ch/pdf/publications/52.pdf), [published DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01615440.2015.1112753) | Measure walking, reaching, kneeling and weapon use separately. |
| Where mass sits matters | Five men walked at 1.25 m/s with mass at different leg/waist locations. Distal leg loads raised metabolic demand more; several loaded conditions retained similar kinematics. [Browning et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17473778/) | Similar silhouettes can hide different effort. Heavy boots deserve distinct swing tuning. |
| Loading need not force shorter steps | In a 12-person experiment at 1.5 m/s, backpack loading increased metabolic cost without detected spatiotemporal changes in either footwear condition. [Dames and Smith](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25985924/) | Do not automatically shorten every loaded walk. |

Evidence applies to the tested people, equipment and tasks. Historical field armor, tournament armor, mail, modern plate carriers and fictional powered suits require separate records.

## 2. Physical model: weight, size and speed

Use SI units: mass kg, length m, time s, force N, torque N·m. Mass is not weight: weight = mass × gravity. Use 9.81 m/s² for Earth examples. Include clothing, padding, weapons, shield and supplies in carried mass.

Record each item's mass, dimensions, center of mass, attachment, joint clearance, stiffness, looseness and surface friction. Material alone does not determine movement: a large hollow shell can be light but obstructive, while a compact weight can be dense and mechanically demanding.

### Whole-body acceleration and stopping

For a simplified straight-line model, acceleration = net horizontal force / total mass. Stopping impulse = mass × change in velocity. **Example:** an 80 kg person plus 25 kg equipment moving at 3 m/s has 315 kg·m/s momentum. A constant 300 N net braking force gives 1.05 s and 1.575 m to stop. Unloaded, the same assumptions give 0.80 s and 1.20 m. Real braking depends on foot placement, muscle capacity and traction; these are not recommended animation durations. [Impulse mechanics](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/9-2-impulse-and-collisions)

At the same speed, translational kinetic energy is ½mv²: the example increases from 360 J to 472.5 J. Greater energy must be managed when stopping. Doubling speed quadruples this energy. [Kinetic energy](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-2-kinetic-energy)

Foot traction imposes a further limit: static friction magnitude cannot exceed approximately μsN in a simple contact model. Increased mass also increases normal load, so mass alone does not imply proportionally worse friction-limited stopping. Mud, slipping, muscle limits and balance can dominate. [Friction](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/6-2-friction)

### Rotation, reach and item size

For a rigid item about a specified pivot, I = Icenter + md². Under a simplified fixed-axis model, angular acceleration = net torque / I. **Example:** a 2 kg point-mass approximation at 0.20 m contributes 0.08 kg·m²; at 0.60 m it contributes 0.72 kg·m², nine times the contribution. This is not nine times slower movement: the arm, other equipment, gravity and available torque also matter. A compact shield held close and the same shield at full reach should have different recovery behavior. [Rotational dynamics](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/10-3-dynamics-of-rotational-motion-rotational-inertia)

Animation inference: put extra preparation and recovery where torque and clearance demand it. Heavy hands or a long weapon may preserve a fast committed action while making redirection harder. Two hands, bent elbows and body rotation change leverage. Never infer swing speed from kilograms alone.

### Body size and scaling

For geometrically similar bodies of the same density scaled by factor s, mass scales as s³ and rotational inertia as s⁵. With the additional assumption of similar muscle stress and leverage, torque capacity scales roughly as s³, so angular acceleration scales roughly as s⁻². These are dimensional estimates, not biological laws or a full gait model. Armor of unchanged thickness instead has mass scaling approximately with surface area, s². Decide which scaling assumption is intended.

For gravity-dominated gait comparison, equal Froude number means v/√(gL) is similar, where L is leg length. This can seed retargeting but cannot predict an individual's speed, strength or fatigue. Greater height does not justify uniformly slowing every limb or scaling all masses the same way. [Human stature and walking study](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/213/23/3972/10061/The-mass-specific-energy-cost-of-human-walking-is)

## 3. Movement reconstruction notes

The following are **animation hypotheses**, to compare with reference footage and character geometry.

| Action | Human coordination to retain | Equipment-specific changes to test |
|---|---|---|
| Idle and looking | Small balance adjustments; coordinated eyes/head/trunk | Helmet clearance and inertia; whole-body turns when neck rotation is obstructed. |
| Walk | Alternating support, weight transfer, pelvis motion and foot clearance | Preserve recognizable gait if fit permits. Tune leg swing effort, arm clearance and contact timing independently. |
| Start and sprint | Push against the ground; coordinate trunk and legs | Adjust acceleration to force capacity and load; distinguish short bursts from endurance. |
| Stop and reverse | Braking steps before stable direction change | Add stopping steps or time only as justified by momentum, traction and balance. |
| Turn | Reposition feet and transfer weight through the turn | Shield/weapon radius and asymmetric load can require different turns left versus right. |
| Reach or strike | Coordinate feet, pelvis, torso, shoulder and hand | Test plate collisions, grip, leverage, follow-through and return to guard. |
| Crouch and kneel | Bring the body over a support base | Inspect tassets, knee plates and ankle clearance; use a different stance if deep flexion collides. |
| Rise from ground | Establish support, shift mass over support, then extend | Choose a get-up strategy that the harness permits; do not assume either helplessness or effortless recovery. |
| Stairs or obstacles | Place foot, transfer load, extend supporting leg | Check toe clearance, helmet visibility, step height and equipment collision. |
| Jump and land | Push-off, flight, contact and energy absorption | Armor does not fall faster. Under equal available take-off energy, added mass lowers take-off speed and height; inspect landing and joint limits. |
| Fatigued movement | Preserve intent while capacity declines | Test slower repeated actions, pauses and recovery breathing; do not add a limp without a character reason. |

Rigid plates should follow their attachment and articulation, not stretch like skin. Flexible layers can deform; loose straps or hanging components may lag and settle. Tune secondary motion from attachment stiffness and damping. Avoid universal exaggerated plate wobble or constant clanking.

## 4. Timing without an invented armor speed penalty

Measure forward speed, cadence and step length separately. For steady walking:

speed (m/s) = step length (m/step) × cadence (steps/min) / 60.

**Constructed replication example:** at 1.20 m/s and 100 steps/min, step length is 0.72 m; a two-step stride takes 1.20 s and covers 1.44 m. At 30 fps, one stride spans 36 frame intervals. Frame 0 is left contact, frame 18 right contact, frame 36 the next left contact. Toe-off and support transitions must be observed or deliberately authored; they are not supplied as measured data here. Use the companion JSON's explicitly synthetic event track as a starting layout.

At a given target speed, a trained wearer may retain similar cadence while spending more energy. If changing cadence, reconcile root distance and step length to prevent sliding. For longer scenes, adjust sustained effort separately from the first few steps.

For a torque-limited, symmetric accelerate/decelerate rotation through angle θ, an idealized time is t = 2√(Iθ/τ), using radians and equal constant net torque magnitude in both phases. Thus time scales with √I only under those assumptions. Real joints are not constant-torque hinges; use the expression for comparisons, then validate the motion.

## 5. Reference and recording protocol

Start with the [University of Geneva armor research video collection](https://mediaserver.unige.ch/index.php/play/90867), which lists treadmill, gait, functional movement and motion-capture demonstrations. These are links to existing references; this package does not contain downloaded video or claim frame-by-frame analysis of those clips.

1. Record an unloaded baseline and the same tasks with the defined equipment. Keep subject, surface, route and camera setup consistent. Capture both matched speed and comfortable self-selected speed; identify which each clip uses.
2. Use front, side and three-quarter views with full feet and equipment visible, a scale reference, known frame rate and synchronization cue. Preserve originals and their permissions. A single view does not establish reliable 3D joint angles.
3. Record idle, walk, start/stop, left/right turns, run, reach, weapon recovery, squat, kneel, get-up and steps. Separate fresh and fatigued conditions; never invent exhaustion from an ordinary clip.
4. Log masses and dimensions, item center of mass, joint fit, actor experience, speed, cadence, support contacts, action duration, surface, slope and uncertainty. Separate actor mass from equipment mass and avoid counting the helmet twice.
5. Annotate timestamped events: first movement, foot contacts, toe-offs, maximum reach, collision, reversal, stable end pose. Mark occlusion or uncertain events. Label every value observed, measured, calculated or authored.
6. Replicate first on a human baseline rig. Fit armor and attachments; check joint travel and contact. Then adjust local motion and timing. Compare side-by-side at the original frame rate before retargeting to a new body.
7. Preserve source motion plus retargeted motion. Record skeleton mapping, units, axis convention, root motion, frame range, contact events, equipment attachment transforms and version. Use FBX/BVH/glTF as supported by the pipeline, with notes on any lost channels.

For physical recording, have a competent performer choose tasks appropriate to the actual equipment and setting; dangerous stunt performance is not necessary to establish basic gait references.

## 6. Reuse checklist and handoff

- Recompute body/equipment mass and inertia when size, equipment, grip or attachment changes.
- Validate joint clearance, planted feet, balance transitions and equipment collisions.
- Keep skill, strength, intent, fatigue and armor restriction as separate controls.
- Do not apply a universal speed multiplier or reuse a different harness's maximum joint angles.
- Save the input record, sources and authored assumptions beside each motion asset.

Suggested future instruction: “Use this armor movement reference and character-motion-template.json. Fill unknown measurements explicitly, retain human coordination, calculate load and leverage, derive local timing changes, and distinguish evidence from authored choices. Deliver contact annotations and review acceleration, stopping, turns, joint clearance and fatigue before final animation.”
